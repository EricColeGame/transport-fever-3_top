## Cargo Logistics and Multi-Tier Supply Chains

Freight transport in Transport Fever 2 operated on a push-demand system: factories produced commodities only when an explicit downstream consumer signaled a deficit. While functional, it often led to jarring boom-or-bust cycles where a minor track disruption collapsed an entire line's profitability.

The cargo model redesign stands out as one of the defining **transport fever 3 differences**. Industries now support intermediate regional stockpiling, local warehousing, and fractional delivery contracts.

### Step-by-Step Evolution of Cargo Routing

1. **Local Buffering:** Industries feature baseline warehouse capacity, preventing instant production shutdown if a delivery train arrives slightly behind schedule.
2. **Consolidated Freight Depots:** Multi-commodity distribution centers allow diverse goods to pool at unified logistical nodes outside metropolitan limits.
3. **Last-Mile City Logistics:** Municipalities implement low-emission delivery requirements, encouraging players to establish ring-road transfer stations rather than routing heavy 40-ton diesel trucks straight into downtown retail zones.
4. **Supply Chain Interdependence:** Advanced consumer goods require secondary byproducts (such as packaging materials or specialized machinery components), rewarding modular freight networks.

| Supply Chain Attribute | Transport Fever 2 | Transport Fever 3 |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Production Scaling** | Fixed upgrade tiers (100 -> 200 -> 400) | Continuous capacity curves based on fulfillment |
| **Storage Flexibility** | Single-station catchment only | Regional hubbing via secondary distribution hubs |
| **Cargo Spoilage / Decay** | None | Time-sensitive values on perishable goods |
| **Urban Distribution** | Direct street-level dumping | Tiered commercial drop-off and cargo zoning |

These supply adjustments force players to think like actual supply-chain managers. Establishing intermediate sorting yards and branch-line feeder services is now financially viable and often necessary for high-tier production chains.

## Strategic Tips for Adapting to the New Systems

Adapting to the major **transport fever 3 differences** requires setting aside some old habits formed in earlier titles. Here is how to hit the ground running:

- **Avoid Monolithic Single-Track Freight Trunklines:** Dynamic signaling lets you run mixed-traffic double tracks earlier in the timeline without catastrophic deadlocks.
- **Design Station Necks with Future Throat Space:** Leave at least two train lengths of space between your primary terminal switch ladder and the main double-track trunk.
- **Embrace Freight Consolidation Yards:** Do not run separate 10-car trains for every individual industry. Bring lumber, grain, and ore to a central transfer yard, then run massive 20-car block trains across long transit corridors.
- **Monitor Timetable Slack:** When scheduling tight passenger lines, always include 5–8% schedule padding at terminal turnarounds to absorb minor delays caused by crossing traffic.
